## Cheat sheet (the big picture)

| Age / situation | What the adult does |
|---|---|
| **Under ~3, acting on his own** | No need to stop him (no ḥinukh yet), **except** melakha he does because he knows it helps the adults (lights, fire) |
| **From ~3** | Stop him from doing issurim; start bringing him to kiddush, berakhot, tefilla |
| **From ~6–7 (gil ḥinukh)** | Real ḥinukh: explain the reasons, require positive mitzvot |
| **Any age, done *by* an adult** | Never feed a child issur or tell him to do issur (even a day-old baby: Torah prohibition) |
| **Someone else's child** | Stop a Torah violation; a d'rabbanan one you can let go (tell the parents if it keeps happening) |

**Leniencies:** a child who really needs something is like a **choleh she-ein bo sakana** (a non-Jew may cook or turn on a light for him; medicine is ok) · under pressure you may tell a child to do a **d'rabbanan** (it's a shvut di-shvut) · **never** a d'oraita.

**Games:** board games, dice, tops, Lego, paper planes, puzzles ✓ (kids) · writing, cutting, gluing, Play-Doh, musical toys, balloons with knots, battery toys ✗ · sports balls, bicycles ✗ · tricycles ✓ · scooters/skates: better not.

## 1. The mitzva of ḥinukh

- **D'oraita:** teach your children Torah ("*ve-limadtem otam et beneichem*", Devarim 11:19), and the point of learning is doing ("*u-shmartem la'asotam*", Devarim 5:1). So there's a Torah mitzva to teach Torah **and** to get them used to mitzvot in general.
- The child's actual performance of a specific mitzva is **d'rabbanan**.
- Each mitzva starts when the child understands it and can do it properly:
    - **Tzitzit**: once he can put them on correctly and make the berakha.
    - **Tefillin**: only shortly before bar mitzva (needs a clean body and focus) (Sukka 42a; MB 343:3).
- **Gil ḥinukh ≈ 6–7**, when serious Torah learning starts.
- But we **start the habit at ~3**: berakhot, tefilla, coming to hear kiddush and havdala (BB 21a; SA YD 245:5).
- Once he understands Shabbat and can listen properly, make sure he hears kiddush/havdala; if he missed it, he says it himself.

## 2. Ḥinukh for negative commandments

- From when he understands that some things are **always** forbidden (not just "stop because Abba said no"), usually **~3**, stop him from issurim (eating treif, turning on a light) (MB 343:3).
- At 6–7, explain the principles so he can keep them properly.
- **Under 3, acting on his own** → no obligation to stop him (eating something treif he found; a little kohen walking into a cemetery).
- **But an adult may never *cause* a child to sin**, even a day-old baby. That's a **Torah** prohibition (Yevamot 114a; MB 343:4). Can't feed him blood or bugs, can't bring a young kohen to tum'a. Can't even feed him **d'rabbanan** issur (SA 343:1).
    - Handing him something he *might* misuse is ok: paper on Shabbat even though he may tear the letters.
    - Putting treif food **in his hand** = feeding him (MB 340:14).
- **Shabbat has an extra issur:** "*lo ta'aseh khol melakha, ata u-vinkha u-vitekha*" (Shemot 20:10). If my child does melakha because he thinks I want it and I don't protest → I'm missing ḥinukh **and** violating a Torah prohibition. If he does it for another Jew who sees and doesn't object → that Jew violates d'rabbanan (SHT 334:54).
- ✓ **Food before kiddush/havdala or on a fast:** a hungry child, even past gil ḥinukh, may be fed. The issur is only food that is itself treif, not kosher food forbidden because of the time (MB 269:1; 6:9).

## 3. Who has to educate and protest?

- Most poskim: the detailed obligation (insisting on mitzvot, stopping aveirot) is on the **father**, like teaching Torah. (Terumat Ha-deshen: father and mother equally.)
- The **mother** still has a general mitzva to educate (ahavat Yisrael, truth). If there's no father (passed away, away), she takes over fully (Eliya Rabba 640:4).
- Practical: a 6–7-year-old who won't stop playing to hear kiddush or bentch → the father must insist. The mother can sometimes let it slide to keep the home pleasant.
- If parents let a child violate **Torah** law, the beit din / education officials should rebuke the father; not for d'rabbanan.
- **Someone else's child (6–7+):**
    - Rambam/SA 343:1: only the father is obligated. Tosafot/Rosh/Rema: everyone.
    - In practice (Ḥayei Adam; MB 343:7): **Torah** issur (turning on a light, laundry, treif) → stop him. **D'rabbanan** (chicken with milk, playing with muktzeh) → don't have to.
    - If he keeps repeating even a d'rabbanan, tell the parents.
- If a child is about to **hurt someone or damage property** → stop him (hashavat aveida, Devarim 22:2; "*lo ta'amod al dam re'ekha*", Vayikra 19:16).
- **Do ḥinukh so it's received.** Don't force a 6-year-old to keep everything and say all the tefillot. The years until bar/bat mitzva are for getting used to it gradually.

## 4. A child turning lights on and off

- Lights went out and the child understands his parents would be happy if he turned them on → **they must tell him not to**, even if he's **under 3**. On Shabbat the rule is stricter: if he understands it helps them, he's doing it for them (Shemot 20:10).
- Same at a neighbor's: if my kid goes to turn on their light, they must stop him.
- **Fire:** a child trying to put it out must be stopped (Shabbat 121a; SA 334:25; MB 66).
- Obviously you can't **tell** a child to turn on a light or put out a fire.
- Child turned a light off by mistake → **don't yell**, or he'll "fix" it by turning it back on. If he wants to fix it, tell him not to.
- **Benefiting:** child did melakha **for an adult** → others can't benefit; **for himself** → others may (BHL 325:10).

## 5. Leniencies in a pressing situation

- **Never** tell a child to do a **d'oraita** (causing a child to sin + "*u-vinkha*").
- **D'rabbanan for the child's own benefit:** Rashba and Ran permit; most Rishonim forbid (Rambam, Tosafot, SA 343:1). **Be-sha'at ha-dechak** rely on the lenient view (R. Akiva Eger; BHL 343:1).
    - Light left on in his room and he can't sleep → can tell him to turn it off (that's d'rabbanan). Better a child under 6; over 6, better with a shinui.
- **D'rabbanan not for the child's benefit, under pressure:** also ok. A child's whole Shabbat obligation is d'rabbanan, so his d'rabbanan is a **shvut di-shvut**, which is permitted for a mitzva or great need (9:11). Only **occasionally**, so he doesn't learn to take Shabbat lightly.
- **A baby (~1 year)** who has no idea what a switch does: if a needed light went out, can hold him near the switch hoping he plays with it, and take him away once it's on. He's a **mitasek** (26:3) (Rashba; Orḥot Shabbat 24:7–8).

## 6. A child is like a sick person

- Normally you can't ask a non-Jew to do melakha. But a child who **really needs** something is like a **choleh she-ein bo sakana** → can ask a non-Jew: cook for a hungry child with no food, turn on a light he really needs (Rema 276:1; MB 6; 28:2).
- More lenient with babies, but also for needy older kids.
- **Medicine**: a child may take it even for mild pain (the gezeira on refu'ot doesn't apply, as with a sick person).
- **Diaper cream**: place it, **don't spread** it. If the diaper then spreads it, no problem (14:5; 28:8).

## 7. Permitted and forbidden games

- Mitzva to train kids to **learn Torah** a lot on Shabbat and play less, more so as bar/bat mitzva approaches. Great for parents to learn with them; communities should run kids' shiurim.
- The rules below are for **children**. **Adults:** le-khatchila no games at all (22:13); even those who allow it are strict on the problem cases below.
- ✓ Checkers, chess, memory, dice, spinning tops.
- ✗ Games with a prize of **money or food**; games that normally involve **writing** (SA 338:5, 322:6).
- **Monopoly** (fake money/property): some say avoid. Kids may be lenient (SSK 16:33); adults should not.
- ✗ Writing, gluing, cutting, weaving games.
- **Jigsaw puzzles; making words from letter tiles**: kids may (letters are just placed temporarily, 18:4); adults strict.
- ✗ Precise plastic models meant to last. ✓ Kids: **Lego**-type blocks (build and take apart), paper planes and boats. Adults strict (15:7).
- **Tents:** ✗ draping a blanket over chairs. ✓ Hold the blanket up first, then slide the chairs under. Same with blocks: a house/garage with a tefaḥ-square inside only if you hold the roof up and build the walls under it (15:5).
- **Jewelry kits** (not made to last) → ok, but close with a **bow**, not a regular knot (SSK 16:22).
- **Mixed-up game pieces:** don't sort them (Borer). Taking out the pieces you need to **play now** is fine; that's how a game starts (*derekh misḥak*, 11:16).
- ✗ **Play-Doh / modeling clay**: Memare'aḥ (SA 314:11), plus Kotev if the shapes mean something → **muktzeh**.

## 8. More games and the yard

- **Wind-up car** (compress the spring) → ok, if no sound or lights. ✗ **Battery toys** (17:2).
- **Balloons:** ✗ inflate (you tie a knot). With a **valve** and already inflated before → ok (15:8).
- **Musical toys** (trumpet, piano, guitar, bells, noisemakers) ✗ and muktzeh. ✓ Can **give a baby** a toy that makes noise when shaken or pressed, but the adult can't make it sound (MB 338:1).
- **Sand:** muktzeh unless set aside for the kids before Shabbat (23:3). Then ok only if it's **fine and dry** (no shaping). ✗ Wet sand you can dig and fill (Boneh); ✗ wetting sand (Lash, 15:2).
- ✗ **Marbles**/games needing flat ground outside (you may level it), even on paved ground (you may move to dirt). ✓ Indoors on the floor.
- ✓ **Apricot pits**: kids use them as toys, so not muktzeh, even ones taken out on Shabbat.
- **Swings:** ✓; ✗ if hung from a tree, even on one side; ✓ if hung from a peg driven into the tree (19:7).

## 9. Ball games and running

- ✗ **Soccer, football, baseball, basketball, tennis**: adults make a big deal of them with complicated rules → *uvda de-ḥol*. Plus they lead to field prep, driving, registering, tickets. The balls are **muktzeh**, even at home or in the yard.
- ✗ **Watching** a game.
- ✓ Kids may run and play for fun; ✗ exercise classes (22:8).
- ✓ **Little kids' balls**, indoors or on a **paved** yard; ✗ on grass or dirt (leveling). No worry they'll keep it up as adults, since adults don't play with those balls.
- ✓ **Ping-pong** for fun (played indoors).
- ✗ Getting a ball out of a tree by hand or stick (might break a branch). If it falls out by itself → ok (19:7; SSK 16:8).

## 10. Bikes, scooters, skates

- ✗ **Bicycle**, even with training wheels: weekday activity (22:8).
- ✓ **Tricycle** for little kids: really different from a bicycle, so not a weekday activity.
- **Scooters & skates:**
    - Lenient: like running.
    - Strict: running is ok because it's unassisted; equipment that makes you move faster = weekday activity.
    - Rav Melamed: the lenient have someone to rely on be-di'avad, but **better to be strict**. Same reasoning as the custom not to ride bikes. Also: keeping little kids to simpler games teaches the older ones that Shabbat is for Torah and rest.
