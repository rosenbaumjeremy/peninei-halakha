## Cheat sheet (the big picture)

| Topic | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|
| **Pat akum** | Any bread or mezonot baked by a gentile; a Jew lighting/adjusting the oven permits it |
| **Pat palter** | Abroad, where no Jewish baker: permitted if ingredients are certified; not in Israel |
| **Bishul akum** | Food not eaten raw + fit for a royal table |
| **Exempt** | Dairy, fruit, most vegetables, snacks, sweets, coffee, alcohol; salted/pickled/smoked |
| **Jewish participation** | Ashkenazim: lighting the fire suffices. Sephardim: placing the food on it |
| **Utensils** | Need kashering (even earthenware, 3× hagala); food made bediavad is permitted |

## 1. Bread

- Stricter: even poor-quality bread is forbidden (it's the staple). More lenient overall (pat palter) because of the need.
- Includes bread baked at home or in a bakery, and cakes/crackers (they're bread for ḥalla). Corn/rice breads: like cooked food.
- **Jewish participation**: lighting the oven, adding a twig, or just lowering and raising the flame → permitted.

## 2. Pat palter

- Hard to keep; Rebbi wondered "how lovely this bread is, why forbid it?" and "is there no baker here?"
- R. Yehuda Nesia: "a court can't permit three decrees" (he already permitted oil). The bread decree stayed vague.
- Practice: where there's **no Jewish baker**, buy from a gentile baker (ingredients kosher). Not where Jewish bakers exist, like Israel.

## 3. The limits

- Abroad, those who want may buy pat palter, including cakes and pastries, if ingredients are kosher.
- Intent matters: a baker's bread for his family → forbidden; anyone's bread baked for sale → permitted.
- A Jewish baker arrives → leniency ends (unless he can't supply everything, lacks a type, or the gentile's is better). In Israel: forbidden.
- Today's bread has dozens of additives (pig/calf fat, insect products) → rabbis must check before permitting.

## 4. The stringent; travelers

- Many abroad were strict; even they ate pat palter for Shabbat meals.
- Strict host, guest who isn't: if the gentile bread is nicer, host breaks it (one says the berakha for all). If each says his own, the host uses Jewish bread.
- Eating with friends who aren't strict: may join, so as not to seem superior.
- Jewish bread within 18 minutes → get it; on the road within 72 minutes → wait.
- Israelis abroad = like the strict, lenient in need. Everyone: strict during the **Ten Days of Repentance**.

## 5. Bishul akum

- Even kosher food in kosher pots cooked by a gentile is forbidden. "Cooking" = any heat: frying, roasting, baking, steaming, microwave.
- Salting, pickling, cold smoking → permitted.
- Only for important foods: (1) **not eaten raw** (the main rule today); (2) **fit for a royal table**.

## 6. Not eaten raw

- Forbidden: meat, fish, grain dishes (pasta, ptitim), legumes (lentils, chickpeas, rice), eggs.
- Permitted: **dairy** (pasteurizing doesn't count), fruit (jam), spices, most vegetables (matbuḥa).
- Potatoes, zucchini, beets, eggplant: forbidden, unless people around you sometimes eat them raw.
- Judged before cooking: flour isn't eaten raw → forbidden. Salted fish/meat already edible → permitted. Mixtures: follow the main ingredient.

## 7. Fit for a royal table

- Served at a respectable meal (main dish or dessert). Today almost everything qualifies.
- Not included: candy, chocolate, nuts/seeds, snacks.
- **Breakfast cereal** and rice cakes: forbidden (eaten for a meal).
- **Coffee and alcohol**: permitted (mostly water; berakha is shehakol; not part of a meal).

## 8. Jewish participation

- A Jew who put it on the fire, lit or raised the fire, or even stirred to improve it → kosher.
- **Ashkenazim**: a Jew lighting/adjusting the fire covers everything cooked on it later.
- **Sephardim** (SA): only a Jew placing the food or adding fire during the cooking (frying: a Jew puts each piece in).
- Rav Melamed: lekhatḥila guide all to be strict, bediavad all may be lenient. Restaurants without personal contact: regular hekhsher lenient; **mehadrin** only strict.

## 9. Commercial cooking; hired help

- Some extend "palter" to restaurant cooks; most poskim don't (only bread). In need, with other reasons, one may rely on them.
- A gentile cooking in a Jewish home: still forbidden (practical ruling).

## 10. The sick and their caregivers

- Shabbat: a gentile may cook for a non-dangerously ill person, and they may eat it. Leftovers after Shabbat: most later authorities forbid even to the patient.
- Gentile caregiver: the patient should light the fire if able; otherwise family should cook and let the caregiver reheat. In great need the patient may rely on lenient views; visitors may not eat it.

## 11. Utensils

- Pots a gentile cooked in need kashering (Rashba, Ran); some say not (Rosh). Halakha: kasher.
- Food cooked in them bediavad is permitted; the pot still needs kashering.
- **Earthenware**: hagala 3× works here (rabbinic issue).

> Line to remember: a Jewish hand on the flame turns their cooking into ours.
