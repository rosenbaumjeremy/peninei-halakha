## Cheat sheet (the big picture)

| Topic | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|
| **Ḥelev** | Certain fats of cattle, sheep and goats (offered on the altar): karet. Not wild animals or birds |
| **Ḥelev vs. shuman** | Ḥelev: a separate layer under a peelable membrane. Shuman: fat covered by meat, permitted |
| **Gid ha-nasheh** | Sciatic nerve on the hip of cattle and wild animals (not birds): Torah prohibition, lashes |
| **Rabbinic additions** | Branches, surrounding fat, the "outer" nerve, threads and membranes |
| **Nikur** | Learned only by watching an expert; many communities sell the hindquarters |
| **Jerusalem nikur** | Combines all communities' stringencies; the standard for public hekhsherim |

## 1. The prohibition of ḥelev

- Ḥelev is burned on the altar and blood sprinkled on it, so Israel may not eat them (Vayikra 3:17).
- Applies to **ox, sheep and goat** (offered as sacrifices). Not to wild animals (ḥaya) or birds.
- Forbidden to eat, but **benefit** and trade are permitted.
- Most forbidden foods carry lashes; ḥelev and blood carry **karet**: eating what belongs to the altar degrades the holy.

## 2. What is ḥelev?

- Ḥelev = large, firm pieces of fat in three places, easy to remove whole. Shuman = fat mixed into the meat, eaten with a shelamim.
- Once cut, they're hard to tell apart: the classic case for an **asham talui** (Keritot 4:1).
- "Ḥelev" means the choicest part ("the fat of the land"; Hevel's offering "from their fat").
- Three types: on the **innards** (stomachs), on the **kidneys**, and on the **flanks** (Vayikra 3:3–4).

## 3. Ḥelev in practice

- Two signs: it's spread like a cloth with a **membrane** that peels off with it (Ḥullin 49b); fat that meat **covers** is shuman and permitted (93a).
- Rabbis also forbade fat attached to ḥelev, and threads/membranes drawing from it (in spleen, flanks, kidneys).
- Exile created doubts about more fats; Torah doubts → strict; rabbinic doubts → usually strict too, given how severe ḥelev is.
- Removed ḥelev goes with the terefot so it won't mix back in.

## 4. Gid ha-nasheh

- Don't eat the sciatic nerve "on the hollow of the thigh" (Bereishit 32:33), on both legs, in **cattle and wild animals**.
- Birds are exempt: their thigh is flat, not rounded like a "kaf."
- The Torah-forbidden part: up to ~**8 cm** in a big ox, ~4 cm in a sheep; easy to remove.
- Rabbinic: the rest of the nerve, its branches (**kenokanot**), the "outer" nerve; custom: the surrounding fat.
- The nerve itself has no taste but is still forbidden (lashes). Branches and fat do have taste → a dish tasting of them is forbidden. Benefit permitted.

## 5. Yaakov's struggle

- Yaakov went back across the Yabbok alone for **small jugs** (Ḥullin 91a: the righteous value honestly earned money).
- A "man" wrestled him till dawn, struck his hip, then blessed him: "Yisrael." He limped as the sun rose.
- Not eating it keeps his legacy alive: with devotion and strength he built the family of Israel and brought it home.
- The mitzva was given at Sinai, attached to the story to teach its reason; Yaakov's family likely kept it earlier by intuition.

## 6. The deeper meaning

- The angel was **Esav's angel**: the world should run by nature, vs. Yaakov's vision of fixing it through faith and Torah.
- The nerve links head and heart (ideas, feelings) to the legs (action). Esav's claim: ideals sound nice but can't work in the real world.
- "Nasheh" = weakness, forgetting: ideas weaken on the way to action, near the seat of desire.
- The small jugs = the weak and sinners of Israel, whom Yaakov wouldn't give up. The blow struck the **people of action** who support Torah scholars (Zohar).
- Like the **arava**: no taste, yet essential. In the end the sun rose; the link between spirit and action will be restored.

> Line to remember: we skip the gid ha-nasheh to say ideals can and must reach all the way down to the feet.

## 7. The process of nikur

- **Nikur** = removing ḥelev and gid ha-nasheh. Rema: learned only by **watching** an expert.
- Exile broke the chain of tradition → more doubts → more stringencies (Tur on the Ittur: "the stringent one is blessed").
- Once, everyone (often **women**) did nikur and was trusted, as with sheḥita (Rabbeinu Yeruḥam).
- Maharshal: today only a **God-fearing expert**, since people can't tell which rule is Torah or rabbinic.

## 8. The hindquarters

- Many communities sold the **hindquarters** (where most ḥelev and the gid are) to non-Jews: Radbaz in Egypt, Shela, much of Europe. Not binding.
- About half the meat, including choice cuts, but heavy nikur ruins it anyway (many cuts, soaking) and takes 1–2 hours per leg, leaving mostly ground meat.
- Some sell most of it but kasher the prime cuts (fillet, sirloin).

## 9. Jerusalem nikur

- ~150 years ago, Ashkenazi rabbis in Jerusalem set a method combining **all** communities' stringencies; it spread everywhere.
- Loses ~13–25% of hindquarter weight. Yemenite/Moroccan traditions lose ~5–10%; one may rely on them, but public hekhsherim follow Jerusalem.
- When the Temple is rebuilt, the Great Beit Din will have to decide exactly what ḥelev is, since the rest must be eaten.
